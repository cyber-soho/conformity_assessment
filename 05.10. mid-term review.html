Chapter 1 — Introduction to conformity assessment

1.1 Definition and objectives

A conformity assessment is the demonstration that specified requirements are fulfilled. The definition comes from ISO/IEC 17000:2020, the vocabulary standard of the International Organization for Standardization (ISO). Three words carry the meaning: demonstration (you show it with evidence, you do not assert it), specified requirements (something written, from a law, a contract, a standard or a policy) and fulfilled (a comparison with a yes or no result).

Every assessment, large or small, follows the same five links. The course calls this the assessment chain:

  1. Requirement — a need or expectation that is stated, generally implied or obligatory. Example: "company laptops lock after 15 minutes of inactivity" (Acceptable Use Policy §4.2 and the CIS Windows 11 Benchmark recommendation of 900 seconds or fewer).
  2. Control — the measure that is supposed to satisfy the requirement. Example: the Windows registry value InactivityTimeoutSecs.
  3. Test — the planned procedure applied to the control: reading the value and comparing it with 900 seconds.
  4. Evidence — the verifiable record produced by the test: a dated text file naming the machine, the value observed and the person who collected it.
  5. Finding — the evaluation of the evidence against the requirement: conformity, non-conformity or observation.

Vocabulary you must be able to tell apart:

Term Meaning Memory point
Conformity A requirement is fulfilled Result of a test
Non-conformity, major The control is absent or has broken down (no backup exists at all) Absent / broken
Non-conformity, minor An isolated lapse in a control that otherwise works (one laptop out of ten with a 20-minute lock) Isolated lapse
Observation Not a non-conformity, but a weakness or an improvement opportunity (a firewall rule without a comment) Weakness, not a failure
Correction Fixes the single instance (encrypt the one unencrypted laptop) The instance
Corrective action Removes the cause so the problem does not come back (enforce encryption on every deployment) and is verified afterwards The cause
Audit trail The chronological record (files, timestamps, names) that lets another person reconstruct how a finding was reached Reconstructable
Conformity vs compliance Conformity = fulfilling a requirement of any origin (ISO usage). Compliance = fulfilling mandatory external obligations (laws, regulations, contracts). Érable Cycles must comply with Law 25 and may choose to conform to ISO/IEC 27001. Mandatory → comply

The three objectives of a conformity assessment: determine whether each requirement in scope is fulfilled, demonstrate the result with evidence a third person can verify, and enable decisions (correct, accept, certify). An auditor never fixes anything during a test — the auditor observes and reports; the administrator corrects.

The four sources of requirements, from the most binding to the least: laws and regulations; contracts (the PCI DSS merchant agreement); standards and frameworks adopted voluntarily; the organization's internal policies. An auditor's personal preference and a vendor's brochure are not requirements.

1.2 Why conformity assessment matters in cybersecurity management

Five practical reasons were given in class:

  1. Due diligence — the reasonable, documented steps a prudent organization takes to protect the information it holds. A dated assessment report with its evidence is the proof of it; an e-mail from the CEO (Chief Executive Officer) saying "security is a priority" is an assertion, not a proof.
  2. Legal and financial exposure known before an incident — which obligations apply, who enforces them, what the penalties are and how fast you must notify.
  3. Facts for risk management — observed control states replace opinions in the likelihood estimates.
  4. Faster answers to customers — security questionnaires are answered from a current assessment.
  5. Measured improvement — a security posture is measured at one moment and compared with the previous assessment (findings → corrective actions → verification → next assessment).

The exposure of Érable Cycles comes from five facts, each of which triggers one obligation:

Company fact Obligation it triggers Who enforces it Notification clock after an incident

Operates in Québec Law 25 (the modernized Private Sector Act) Commission d'accès à l'information du Québec (CAI) "Promptly"

A regulator is a public body that enforces a law. A contractual obligation binds through a contract rather than a law — PCI DSS reaches the merchant through the merchant agreement with the acquiring bank. The SAQ (Self-Assessment Questionnaire) is the PCI DSS validation form a small merchant completes itself.

1.3 Types of audits and assessments

ISO 19011 defines an audit as a systematic, independent and documented process for obtaining objective evidence and evaluating it objectively to determine the extent to which the audit criteria are fulfilled. The fourth edition, ISO 19011:2026, was published in May 2026 and replaces the 2018 edition; the principles are unchanged.

Who performs the audit decides its type:

Type Performed by Independence Typical output Érable Cycles example
First-party (internal) The organization on itself Lowest Internal report The audit team checks the company against CIS Controls IG1 (Implementation Group 1) under the CEO's mandate
Self-declaration The organization, because a contract requires it First party in form, contractual in origin A signed attestation (the PCI DSS SAQ A) The yearly SAQ A sent to the acquiring bank
Second-party An interested party — usually a customer auditing a supplier Medium Questionnaire result, site-visit report A corporate client sends a 40-question questionnaire before a fleet contract
Third-party An independent body with no stake in the result (certification body, QSA — Qualified Security Assessor) High Certificate or attestation An accredited certification body audits the ISMS (information security management system) for an ISO/IEC 27001 certificate
Regulatory A public authority (OPC, CAI, CRTC) High, plus legal powers Binding orders, penalties The CAI investigates how newsletter consents were collected after a complaint

Attestation is a formal statement that requirements are fulfilled; a declaration is an attestation by the organization itself; a certification is an attestation by a third party. ISO publishes standards and never certifies anyone — accredited certification bodies do, and in Canada they are accredited by the Standards Council of Canada (SCC).

Point-in-time assessment = the state on the day of the test (a single script run, a one-day on-site assessment, even an annual surveillance audit). Continuous monitoring = automatic, repeated re-evaluation between audits (Microsoft Purview Compliance Manager score, Intune compliance policies, AWS Config rules).

The seven audit principles of ISO 19011: integrity · fair presentation · due professional care · confidentiality · independence · evidence-based approach · risk-based approach. "Maximum automation" and "zero findings" are not principles.

1.4 The role of conformity assessment in continuous improvement

The PDCA (Plan–Do–Check–Act) cycle: the conformity assessment is the Check step; corrective actions are the Act step.

ISO/IEC 27001:2022 organizes this in two clauses:

  • Clause 9 — Performance evaluation: 9.1 monitoring, measurement, analysis and evaluation · 9.2 internal audit at planned intervals by objective and impartial auditors · 9.3 management review by top management (audit results, status of corrective actions, risk changes, improvement opportunities).
  • Clause 10 — Improvement: 10.1 continual improvement · 10.2 nonconformity and corrective action — react and correct, look for the root cause, act to remove it, review the effectiveness, update the risks and the ISMS, keep documented information.

A corrective action is finished only when a verification of effectiveness (a re-test) proves that the requirement is now fulfilled. A corrective-action register entry contains the non-conformity, an owner, a due date, the verification method and a re-test date.

Historical comparison compares the same items from one assessment to the next: improved (non-conform → conform), unchanged, regressed (conform → non-conform). A regression is the strongest signal for management because a working control stopped working — usually a process failure (no owner, no schedule), not a technical gap. The conformity rate = items conform ÷ items tested, per domain. Historical comparison is performance criterion 5.1 of competency EZ04.

The six historical items of the case file (last year → this year): H1 disk encryption on the 45 laptops (31 of 45 → 45 of 45, improved) · H2 MFA (multi-factor authentication) on Microsoft 365 (administrators only → everyone, improved) · H3 firewall-rule review within six months (conform both years) · H4 quarterly access review of AD (Active Directory) accounts (still not performed) · H5 backup restore test (done → not done for 14 months, regressed) · H6 newsletter unsubscribe honoured within 10 business days (conform both years).

Chapter 2 — Laws and regulations

2.1 The legal landscape — the hierarchy of obligations and who enforces what

For every requirement an auditor records its source (the document it comes from) and its binding force (how strongly the organization is obliged to meet it). Sorted by binding force, requirements form the hierarchy of obligations:

Level Kind Written by Enforced by Can the organization decline it? Érable Cycles
1 Laws and regulations Parliament of Canada, National Assembly of Québec, EU institutions; regulations by the government under a law Regulators and courts No PIPEDA, Law 25, GDPR, CASL, Income Tax Act
2 Contractual standards An industry body; mandatory because a signed contract requires it The other party to the contract (acquirer, card brands) No — unless it stops accepting cards PCI DSS v4.0.1
3 Voluntary standards and frameworks ISO, NIST, CIS Nobody by law; a certification body or a customer if asked Yes CIS Controls v8.1 IG1, NIST CSF 2.0, ISO/IEC 27001
4 Internal policies Management Management and HR (human resources) Management can change them; staff must follow them Acceptable Use Policy, Privacy Policy

Definitions to know: a law (act, statute) is adopted by a legislature; a regulation (Canadian sense) is a detailed rule adopted by the government under a law and is just as mandatory — the Breach of Security Safeguards Regulations (SOR/2018-64) under PIPEDA add that breach records are kept 24 months. In the EU, a "Regulation" such as the GDPR is a law that applies directly in every member state. A standard is voluntary by itself and becomes mandatory when a contract requires it (that is how PCI DSS reaches a merchant, and how ISO/IEC 27001 becomes a level-2 requirement for a supplier whose customer writes it into the contract). A framework is a structured set of practices the organization chooses. A policy is a formal statement approved by management for its own staff.

Who enforces what:

Body Text Main powers
OPC — federal PIPEDA Investigates complaints, receives breach reports, publishes findings, signs compliance agreements, can go to the Federal Court. It does not fine. Knowingly failing to report a breach or to keep breach records is an offence prosecuted by the Attorney General of Canada — fine up to $100,000.
CAI — Québec Private Sector Act as amended by Law 25 Inspects, investigates, issues orders, imposes AMPs (administrative monetary penalties)
CRTC — federal CASL (messages and computer programs) Investigates, imposes AMPs. The Competition Bureau handles misleading electronic messages; the OPC handles address harvesting.
Card brands and the acquirer PCI DSS (through the merchant agreement) Fees and fines passed on, higher transaction costs, loss of the right to accept cards
Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) Income Tax Act record keeping Audits, reassessments, penalties
EU supervisory authorities (e.g. the CNIL) GDPR Investigations, orders, fines up to €20 million or 4 % of worldwide turnover

Courts sit above all of them. Applicability is decided by the scope of a text: whom it covers, which information or activity, and where. Jurisdiction is the territory and subject matter a law and its regulator cover. Some laws have extraterritorial effect (the GDPR applies to Érable Cycles because it offers goods to people in the EU). In Lab 1 the answer is "Applies? Yes / Partly / No" with one sentence naming the company fact. Legal (compliance) risk = the possibility of sanctions, financial loss or reputational damage because an applicable requirement is not met.

2.2 PIPEDA — the federal private-sector privacy law

PIPEDA (S.C. 2000, c. 5). Part 1 applies to every organization that collects, uses or discloses personal information (information about an identifiable individual: name, home address, e-mail, purchase history, photo) in the course of commercial activities. Information about the business itself is not personal information. Québec, British Columbia and Alberta have substantially similar private-sector laws that govern inside the province; PIPEDA still applies when information crosses a provincial or national border and to federally regulated businesses (banks, airlines, telecommunications). For Érable Cycles both laws apply: Law 25 inside Québec, PIPEDA for information that leaves the province.

The ten fair-information principles (Schedule 1):

No. Principle What an auditor asks Érable Cycles for
4.1 Accountability Name of the privacy officer; approved Privacy Policy
4.2 Identifying purposes Checkout form text, privacy notice
4.3 Consent Opt-in records, consent wording
4.4 Limiting collection Form fields compared with the purposes
4.5 Limiting use, disclosure and retention Retention schedule, deletion logs
4.6 Accuracy Account-update function
4.7 Safeguards BitLocker report, firewall rules, access reviews
4.8 Openness Published Privacy Policy

Meaningful consent (s. 6.1): valid only if the person can reasonably understand the nature, purpose and consequences; the OPC guidelines ask organizations to present the key elements first (what is collected, with whom it is shared, why, any meaningful risk of harm). Express consent = active agreement (ticking an empty box); implied consent = reasonably deduced from the situation. Express consent is generally needed for sensitive information, for a use outside reasonable expectations, or when a meaningful risk of significant harm remains. A pre-ticked box is not an active choice.

Principle 4.7 Safeguards: physical, organizational and technological measures matched to the sensitivity of the information — this is where the law meets the audit tools (disk encryption, firewall profiles, access rights). Principle 4.5 sets a maximum: keep personal information only as long as necessary for its purposes, then destroy, erase or anonymize it.

Breaches of security safeguards (mandatory since November 1, 2018). A breach is the loss of, unauthorized access to or unauthorized disclosure of personal information resulting from a breach of the organization's safeguards or from a failure to establish them. The test that decides what follows is the RROSH (real risk of significant harm), assessed with two factors: the sensitivity of the information and the probability of misuse. Significant harm includes bodily harm, humiliation, damage to reputation or relationships, loss of employment or business opportunities, financial loss, identity theft, negative effects on the credit record, damage to or loss of property.

Duties: contain, assess and document. If there is a RROSH: report to the OPC as soon as feasible (s. 10.1(1), with the OPC breach report form), notify the affected individuals as soon as feasible (s. 10.1(3)), notify other organizations that can reduce the harm, such as the police (s. 10.2). For every breach, with or without RROSH: keep a record (s. 10.3) for 24 months after the day the breach was determined to have occurred (SOR/2018-64, s. 6). Knowingly failing to report, notify or keep records: fine up to $10,000 on summary conviction or $100,000 on indictment (s. 28).

Bill C-36 — tabled June 15, 2026; would enact the Protecting Privacy and Consumer Data Act, repeal Part 1 of PIPEDA and create a Privacy and Consumer Data Commissioner; proposed AMPs up to $10 million or 3 % of gross global revenue and fines up to $25 million or 5 %. Status: at second reading in the House of Commons — a proposal. An auditor assesses against the law in force, PIPEDA. (LEGISinfo page)

2.3 Québec's Law 25 — the modernized Private Sector Act

Law 25 (S.Q. 2021, c. 25, formerly Bill 64) amended the Act respecting the protection of personal information in the private sector (CQLR c. P-39.1). It entered into force in three steps: September 22, 2022 (person in charge, confidentiality-incident rules) · September 22, 2023 (governance policies, privacy impact assessments, privacy by default, consent rules, destruction or anonymization, penalties) · September 22, 2024 (data portability — fully in force since that date). It applies to every enterprise that collects, holds, uses or communicates personal information in Québec, whatever its size, and is enforced by the CAI.

  • Person in charge of the protection of personal information: by default the person with the highest authority (the CEO); delegation in writing; title and contact information published on the website (s. 3.1).
  • Governance policies published in clear and simple terms: roles, retention and destruction, complaint processing (s. 3.2); a privacy policy when information is collected by technological means such as a web store (s. 8.2).
  • PIA (privacy impact assessment) required (a) for any project to acquire, develop or overhaul an information system or electronic service delivery involving personal information (s. 3.3) and (b) before communicating personal information outside Québec — which includes the other Canadian provinces — with a written agreement (s. 17). Moving the newsletter list to a platform hosted in the United States triggers both.
  • Confidentiality incident (s. 3.6): unauthorized access, use or communication, loss, or any other breach of protection. Assess the risk of serious injury with three factors (s. 3.7): sensitivity, anticipated consequences, likelihood of injurious use. If serious: notify the CAI and the persons concerned promptly (s. 3.5). Enter every incident in the register of confidentiality incidents (s. 3.8), kept at least five years after the enterprise became aware of the incident; copy to the CAI on request.
  • Privacy by default (s. 9.1): the highest level of confidentiality without any action by the person (cookies excluded) — a pre-ticked marketing box contradicts it. Data portability (s. 27): on request, in a structured, commonly used technological format. Destruction or anonymization (s. 23) when the purposes are achieved, subject to retention periods fixed by another law. Consent (s. 14): manifest, free, enlightened, for specific purposes, requested for each purpose in clear and simple terms; express for sensitive information.
  • Penalties: AMP by the CAI up to $10 million or 2 % of worldwide turnover, whichever is greater; penal fine by a court from $15,000 up to $25 million or 4 % (doubled for a repeat offence); punitive damages of at least $1,000 when the infringement is intentional or results from gross fault.

Three differences an auditor notices between PIPEDA and Law 25: the regulator (OPC vs CAI), the wording of the test (two factors vs three factors) and the retention of the register (24 months vs 5 years).

2.4 The GDPR — why a European law applies to a Montréal retailer

The GDPR is Regulation (EU) 2016/679, applied since May 25, 2018 in the EU and the EEA (European Economic Area). Vocabulary: personal data relates to an identified or identifiable natural person, the data subject; processing is any operation on personal data; the controller decides the purposes and means (Érable Cycles); the processor processes on the controller's behalf (the MSP, the hosting provider).

Territorial scope — Article 3. Art. 3(1): organizations established in the EU. Art. 3(2): organizations outside the EU that (a) offer goods or services to people in the EU or (b) monitor their behaviour there. Recital 23 gives the signs of an "offer": a language or currency of a member state, delivery to member states, mentions of EU customers. Mere accessibility of a website from Europe is not enough. Érable Cycles delivers to France and Belgium, shows prices in euros and offers a French checkout → Art. 3(2)(a) applies, only to the data of those customers. Lab 1 answer: "Partly".

Article 5 principles: lawfulness, fairness and transparency · purpose limitation · data minimisation · accuracy · storage limitation · integrity and confidentiality · plus accountability (Art. 5(2): the controller must be able to demonstrate compliance). Article 6 — six lawful bases: consent, contract, legal obligation, vital interests, public task, legitimate interests (an order → contract; the newsletter to EU subscribers → consent).

Articles a non-EU online retailer must apply: Art. 27 EU representative designated in writing · Art. 30 records of processing activities (ROPA) — the under-250-employee exemption does not apply because the processing is not occasional · Art. 32 security of processing · Art. 33 notification to the supervisory authority without undue delay and, where feasible, within 72 hours of becoming aware, unless the breach is unlikely to result in a risk; a late notification explains the delay; every breach is documented (Art. 33(5)) · Art. 34 communication to the data subjects when the risk is high · Art. 35 DPIA (data protection impact assessment) before high-risk processing. Without an EU establishment there is no "one-stop shop": the company notifies the authority of each member state concerned (the CNIL in France, the APD-GBA in Belgium), as the European Data Protection Board (EDPB) guidelines explain.

Fines (Art. 83): up to €10 million or 2 % (failures under Arts. 25–39: records, security, breach notification, DPIA, representative) · up to €20 million or 4 % (Art. 5 principles, lawful bases, data-subject rights, transfers). Transfers: the European Commission recognizes Canada as adequate for organizations subject to PIPEDA (adequacy decisions, confirmed in the January 2024 review).

The 72-hour clock runs in calendar hours, weekends included: aware on Saturday at 22:15 Montréal time → deadline Tuesday at 22:15 Montréal time (Wednesday 04:15 in Paris).

2.5 PCI DSS v4.0.1 — the contractual standard for card payments

PCI DSS is published by the PCI Security Standards Council, founded by the major card brands. It is not a law: the card brands require it from the acquirers, the acquirer requires it from the merchant in the merchant agreement. v4.0.1 was published on June 11, 2024 (limited revision of v4.0, no requirement added or removed); v4.0 was retired on December 31, 2024; the future-dated requirements of v4 became mandatory on March 31, 2025. The standard is licensed: download it from the official document library.

Account data = cardholder data (PAN — primary account number, cardholder name, expiration date, service code) + sensitive authentication data (SAD) (full track data, card verification code such as CVV2, PIN data). SAD is never stored after authorization, even encrypted.

Twelve requirements in six goals: (1) secure network and systems — req. 1 network security controls, req. 2 secure configurations · (2) protect account data — req. 3 stored account data, req. 4 strong cryptography in transmission · (3) vulnerability management — req. 5 malware, req. 6 secure systems and software · (4) strong access control — req. 7 need to know, req. 8 identify and authenticate, req. 9 physical access · (5) monitor and test — req. 10 logging and monitoring, req. 11 testing · (6) req. 12 information security policy and programs.

Scope. The CDE (cardholder data environment) = the people, processes and technologies that store, process or transmit cardholder data or SAD. PCI DSS scope = the CDE plus every connected or security-impacting system (a domain controller that manages the accounts of CDE systems). Segmentation keeps other systems out of scope. The cheapest way to reduce the work is to reduce the scope: a hosted payment page (the customer types the card number on the provider's page; the merchant's server never receives it) and P2PE terminals (card data encrypted inside a validated terminal, decrypted only by the solution provider). Érable Cycles: no card numbers stored, hosted page, P2PE → most systems out of scope.

Validation. Large merchants: a ROC (Report on Compliance) written by a QSA. Smaller merchants: an SAQ plus a signed AOC (Attestation of Compliance); some SAQs also require quarterly external scans by an ASV (Approved Scanning Vendor). SAQ types: A (card-not-present, all account-data functions fully outsourced; since January 2025 the merchant also confirms the site is not susceptible to script attacks) · A-EP (e-commerce site that controls the redirection) · B / B-IP (standalone terminals) · C-VT / C (virtual terminal / Internet-connected payment applications) · P2PE (validated P2PE terminals only) · D (everything else, e.g. card data received on the merchant's own server). Érable Cycles today: SAQ A for the web store + SAQ P2PE for the stores; two years ago, with its own card form and stored card numbers, it would have been SAQ D.

2.6 CASL — Canada's Anti-Spam Legislation

CASL (S.C. 2010, c. 23) applies to CEMs (commercial electronic messages) sent to an electronic address (e-mail, text, instant or direct message) when a computer system in Canada is used to send or access them; message rules in force since July 1, 2014, computer-program rules since January 15, 2015. A message is a CEM when one of its purposes is to encourage participation in a commercial activity — the Érable Cycles newsletter is one.

The three requirements of every CEM (section 6): consent (express or implied) · identification (sender, anyone on whose behalf it is sent, contact information valid at least 60 days) · an unsubscribe mechanism (free, easy, valid at least 60 days, given effect without delay and no later than 10 business days after the request — s. 11).

Express consent: active, informed agreement, no expiry until withdrawn; a pre-checked box is not acceptable (CRTC bulletin 2012-548). Implied consent exists only in listed situations, mainly the existing business relationship: a purchase within the past 2 years, an inquiry or application within the past 6 months (CRTC guidance). Proof of consent is on the sender (s. 13): keep the date, the method, the wording and the source. Installing a computer program on someone else's device in a commercial activity requires express consent (s. 8).

Enforcers: the CRTC (messages and programs), the Competition Bureau (false or misleading messages), the OPC (address harvesting). Maximum AMP per violation: $1 million for an individual, $10 million for a business. Recent figures (April 1 – September 30, 2025, CRTC): 152,603 complaints to the Spam Reporting Centre, 153 notices to produce, 123 warning letters, one notice of violation with a $50,000 penalty.

3.1 NIST Cybersecurity Framework 2.0

The NIST Cybersecurity Framework (CSF) 2.0 is voluntary and free, published by NIST (an agency of the United States Department of Commerce) on February 26, 2024 as NIST CSWP 29. Version 1.0 (2014) was written for critical infrastructure; 2.0 is written for any organization of any size. It describes outcomes — not products, not configuration settings.

Three components: the Core (the outcomes), the Tiers (how risk is governed and managed) and the Organizational Profiles (the Core applied to one organization).

The Core: 6 Functions, 22 Categories, 106 Subcategories. Identifiers read Function.Category-Number: GV.PO-01 = Govern, Policy, Subcategory 01; PR.AA-05 = Protect, Identity Management, Authentication and Access Control, 05.

Function Code Covers Where in the course
Govern (new in 2.0, drawn in the centre) GV Strategy, expectations, policy, roles, oversight, supply-chain risk Lab 1, administrative controls
Identify ID Assets, suppliers, vulnerabilities, improvements (ID.AM asset management) Lab 2
Protect PR Access control, awareness, data security, platform security Lab 3
Detect DE Continuous monitoring, adverse event analysis Lab 3
Respond RS Incident management, reporting and communication Project

The Functions are not sequential; Govern informs the other five.

Tiers describe the rigour of risk governance: Tier 1 Partial (ad hoc, reactive) · Tier 2 Risk Informed (management approves practices, not yet organization-wide policy) · Tier 3 Repeatable (formal policy, regularly updated practices) · Tier 4 Adaptive (continuous improvement, predictive indicators). They are not maturity levels to climb for their own sake; an SME (small or medium-sized enterprise) such as Érable Cycles aims at Tier 2 moving towards Tier 3.

Profiles: the Current Profile records what is achieved today (evidence); the Target Profile records what is wanted (laws, contracts, risks, goals); gap = target − current, ranked by size, becomes the action plan. A Profile contains a requirement, the actual situation, a comparison and an action — the same four elements as an audit finding, which is why the course calls it a small conformity assessment. NIST also publishes Community Profiles, Quick-Start Guides (the Small Business Quick-Start Guide, SP 1300) and Informative References (mappings to SP 800-53 Rev. 5, CIS Controls, ISO/IEC 27001) through the OLIR program and the CPRT (Cybersecurity and Privacy Reference Tool).

What the CSF gives Érable Cycles: a common vocabulary with the CEO and the MSP, a structure for the Target Profile, free mappings, a way to show progress year after year. What it does not give: configuration settings, a certification (nobody is "CSF-certified"), a legal obligation. 3.4 Data retention policies — legal minimums, legal maximums and the retention schedule

A retention period is how long a record is kept before destruction or anonymization. A legal minimum says "keep at least this long" (tax and accounting law, some security standards). A legal maximum says "keep no longer than necessary for the purpose" (the privacy laws, for every record containing personal information). A record can be subject to both (an invoice: six-year tax minimum and privacy maximum). When they conflict, the minimum wins for as long as it lasts — the privacy laws themselves except retention periods fixed by another law — then the maximum applies and the record goes. A data retention policy states the rules; a retention schedule applies them record type by record type.

Source Rule Minimum or maximum?
CRA (Income Tax Act, Excise Tax Act) and Revenu Québec Six years from the end of the last tax year the records relate to Minimum
PIPEDA Principle 4.5 Retain only as long as necessary; 4.5.2 guidelines with minimum and maximum periods; 4.5.3 destroy, erase or anonymize Maximum
PIPEDA Breach Regulations (SOR/2018-64) s. 6 Breach record kept 24 months Minimum
Law 25 s. 23 Destroy or anonymize when the purposes are achieved, subject to a period fixed by law Maximum
Law 25 incident regulation Incident register entries kept 5 years Minimum
GDPR Art. 5(1)(e) storage limitation (+ Art. 13 information, Art. 30 ROPA) No longer than necessary in identifiable form Maximum
PCI DSS v4.0.1 req. 3.2.1 Account-data retention and disposal policy; check at least once every three months that expired data is gone Maximum with a verification duty
PCI DSS v4.0.1 req. 10.5.1 Audit logs kept at least 12 months, the most recent three months immediately available Minimum

CCTV (closed-circuit television) — OPC and CAI guidance No fixed statutory period; keep only as long as necessary Maximum

PIPEDA also requires that information used to make a decision about a person be kept long enough for the person to access it — "delete immediately" is wrong too.

The seven columns of a retention schedule: record type (with PI? CHD — cardholder data?) · legal basis (minimum and maximum, article numbers) · retention period (≥ minimum, justified against the maximum) · trigger (the event that starts the clock: end of the tax year, account closure, last activity, recording date) · disposal method (and who confirms it) · owner (business role, not the IT custodian) · exceptions — legal hold (destruction suspended when a dispute, complaint or investigation is reasonably anticipated).

Two retention non-conformities: destroyed too early (below a legal minimum — firewall logs rotated after 30 days when 12 months are required) and kept too long (past the purpose without a legal basis — customer accounts never deleted, orders kept "forever"). Retention is tested with documents and with configuration: /etc/logrotate.conf and journald on Ubuntu, Windows event-log sizes, the backup NAS retention, Microsoft 365 retention policies (Purview Data Lifecycle Management, instructor demo).

3.5 Mapping policies to legal and regulatory requirements — the traceability matrix and crosswalks

A traceability matrix keeps five elements on one line per requirement: requirement → policy clause → control → evidence → test. Columns: Req ID · Source (law or standard and article) · Requirement in plain words · Policy & clause · Control · Evidence to collect · Test (Lab 3) · Framework references (crosswalk). Example R-04: PCI DSS 8.3.6 — passwords of at least 12 characters → Access Control Policy §4.2 (draft) → AD password policy → net accounts output and a Microsoft 365 screenshot → re-performance → CIS 5.2 · CSF PR.AA-01 · ISO A.5.17.

The three gap types, tested in this order:

  1. Policy gap — a requirement with no approved policy clause (a draft policy counts as a gap even when the technical setting is right).
  2. Implementation gap — a clause with no control that enforces it (the Privacy Policy promises deletion after 24 months; no job exists).
  3. Evidence gap — a control exists but leaves no proof (backups run, no restore test record). A control without evidence is treated as not operating.

3.6 Documentation requirements — documents, records, evidence

4.3 Critical assets and their owners

A critical asset is one whose loss, corruption or disclosure would stop or seriously damage a business process the organization cannot do without. "Critical" describes a relation between the asset and a process, not a property of the hardware. The method is the business impact analysis (BIA): list the processes (online sales, in-store sales, customer communication, back office, IT operations and recovery) → rate the impact of an interruption → list the assets each process depends on → an asset inherits the impact of the processes it supports (CSF ID.AM-05).

A single point of failure (SPOF) is a component whose failure stops a process because no alternative exists: the only domain controller, one firewall per site, one EC2 instance and one RDS database, one payment provider for online and in-store payments. Redundancy (a second domain controller, a firewall pair, a database replica) removes the SPOF without changing the impact of the process. The dependencies column records these relations in both directions.

Three roles, two columns: the owner (accountable; decides classification, approves access, defines retention, accepts residual risk — ownership is not legal property: the E-commerce Manager owns the web store database although Amazon runs the hardware) · the custodian (operates and protects according to the owner's decisions) · the user (not recorded, except as identifiers of grouped devices). Two frequent mistakes: making IT the owner of everything (IT can own infrastructure, not the customer database or the finance files) and leaving supplier services without an owner (the contract signatory — Finance and Administration — owns the payment-provider relationship). An empty owner cell is a finding (ISO 5.9, CIS 1.1).

The scoring example: online sales and in-store sales weigh High (3); customer communication, back office and IT operations weigh Medium (2); a SPOF adds 2 points. Results: SRV-DC01, DB-RDS01 and FW-pfSense score 9; the payment provider 8; WEB-APP01 7; the Microsoft 365 tenant 6. The backup NAS scores lowest for day-to-day operations — the owner may override with a written reason because it is essential to recovery. The five critical assets plus the two VMs become the scope of the assessment plan.

4.5 Tagging and categorization — sensitivity levels, legal flags and business impact

Classification assigns a sensitivity level that tells everyone how the asset is handled (ISO 5.12; labelling 5.13; CIS 3.7; PIPEDA 4.7). The owner decides; the assessor checks that the decision was made, follows the scheme and matches the protection.

Level Definition Examples
Public Meant for everyone; disclosure causes no harm (integrity still matters) Web catalogue, store hours, published privacy policy
Internal Staff and contractors only; minor harm Internal procedures, price lists, the lab VMs
Confidential Real harm to the company or individuals; need-to-know; includes personal information Customer accounts and orders, employee records, finance files, e-mail
Restricted Severe harm, legal consequences, or compromises other assets Credentials and keys (domain controller and its backups), security configuration (firewalls), cardholder data at the terminals and the provider

Two legal flags travel independently of the level: PI (personal information → PIPEDA, Law 25, GDPR; drives the PIA, the incident register, the retention schedule and breach duties) and CHD (cardholder data → PCI DSS; inside or attached to the CDE). At Érable Cycles the CHD flag is set only on the P2PE terminals and the payment provider — not on the web store server, because of the hosted payment page.

4.6 Keeping the inventory current

Four practices: one source of truth (one inventory every other list — finance ledger, Intune, the MSP console — is reconciled against, with the source of each column recorded; ISO 5.9 "consistent and aligned") · a review cadence (CIS 1.1 twice a year, PCI DSS 12.5.2 every 12 months, course policy quarterly, 90 days; a review is a dated confirmation by a named person) · change control (every change carries a ticket number written in the record; a change without a ticket is drift — undocumented or unauthorized, a finding either way; decommissioned assets are marked retired with the date, never deleted — ISO 5.11, 7.14) · the inventory as a controlled document (version number bumped at every review, date, reviewer, approval, version history).

5.1 Audit methods and techniques

Audit criteria are the requirements used as the reference (your Lab 1 register). Audit evidence is verifiable information relevant to a criterion. An audit method is the defined way the auditor collects evidence; the audit finding is the result of comparing evidence with the criterion (conformity, nonconformity, observation). A finding without evidence is an opinion.

The six methods of the course:

Method What the auditor does Evidence produced Example
Document review Reads policies, procedures, records Document or record extract Is the Patch Management Procedure approved and dated?
Interview Asks the people who perform the activity Interview notes The IT Administrator explains how critical patches are approved
Walkthrough Follows one real case from start to end with the person who performs it Notes and the documents of that case One new-employee request, from the HR form to the AD account
Observation Watches an activity or a setting as it happens Notes, screenshot The nightly backup job starts and ends successfully
Sampling Tests a subset with a stated rule Sample list with the reason of each pick 10 of the 45 laptops checked for patch level
Re-performance (technical test) Runs the check or the tool personally Tool or script output Get-HotFix run by the auditor on LAB-WIN11

Three references, one vocabulary: NIST SP 800-53A Rev. 5 — three methods, examine, interview, test, each with depth (basic, focused, comprehensive rigour) and coverage (how many and which objects); release 5.2.0 of August 27, 2025 · the PCI DSS v4.0.1 testing procedures use examine, observe, interview · ISO 19011:2026 describes document review, interviews, observation and sampling and adds a defined term for remote auditing methods.

Sampling: the population is the complete set of items a requirement applies to (45 laptops); the sample is the part tested; one item is a sampling unit. Judgement-based sampling selects with knowledge of risk (the three laptops with administrator rights) — efficient, not statistically generalizable. Statistical sampling gives every item a known chance of selection (random); the result can be extended to the population with a measured confidence. A sampling plan records population, method, size, selection rule and the reason of every pick; a random generator with a fixed seed (20261107) gives the same picks every time, so another auditor can verify the selection. Sampling risk is the risk that the sample leads to a different conclusion than testing 100 %. PCI DSS v4.x: the assessor tests 100 % or follows a defined sampling methodology, and the sample must cover every variant of the population (PCI SSC FAQ on sampling). The MC-03 plan: 10 of 45 = 5 judgement (J1 three administrator laptops, J2 one laptop per site with the oldest update — LT-031, LT-044) + 5 random with seed 20261107; coverage 22 %.

Evidence quality: sufficient, reliable, relevant. Reliability increases when evidence comes from an independent source, is an original, is generated by the system rather than typed by a person, and is obtained directly by the auditor. Corroboration confirms one source with a second, independent source (the administrator says the firewall is on → the auditor runs Get-NetFirewallProfile). The ISO 19011 audit life cycle: initiate, prepare, conduct, report, complete, follow up — the plan is "prepare", Lab 3 is "conduct", the Project is "report" and "follow up".

5.2 Selection and application of appropriate tests

A test compares the actual state with an expected result for one requirement. Five parts before execution: the requirement (criterion) · the method · the sample · the expected result (pass rule) · the evidence to keep. After execution: the actual result, the verdict (PASS, FAIL, N/A with a reason, Not tested with a reason) and the evidence reference. A test is repeatable when another auditor obtains the same result.

The verb of the requirement points to the method: "documented / approved / published" → document review (corroborate with an interview of the owner) · "performed / reviewed every N months" → records review with sampling (corroborate with an interview) · "configured / enabled / disabled" → re-performance (corroborate with the baseline document) · "only approved ports reachable" → reachability test with Nmap plus firewall-rule review · "personnel are aware" → interviews corroborated by observation.

Automated tests (CIS-CAT Lite, Lynis, scripts) are fast, repeatable, check 100 % of the settings they know and produce files that can be fingerprinted; their limits: false positives (a problem reported that does not exist — a setting enforced by another mechanism), false negatives (a real problem missed) and limited coverage — CIS-CAT Lite assesses Windows 10/11 Enterprise, Ubuntu 20.04 and Google Chrome, so Lab 3 uses Lynis for Ubuntu 24.04. Manual tests remain necessary for policies, people, physical controls and for interpreting tool output.

Test of design (the control as described would meet the requirement: the procedure requires security updates within 30 days) versus test of operating effectiveness (the control actually operated over a period: the update history shows one security update every month, each within 30 days). One screenshot proves existence at one moment, not operation over time.

The expected result must be measurable: "patching is good" cannot be tested; "the newest security update was installed at most 30 days before the test date" can (PCI DSS 6.3.3: critical patches within one month). Evidence handling: one naming rule (L3-E07_MC-03_hotfix_LAB-WIN11.csv), the collection date, a SHA-256 (Secure Hash Algorithm, 256-bit) fingerprint (64 hexadecimal characters; any change to one byte changes it — integrity, not secrecy), masking of real personal information, one line per file in the evidence index. Test once, satisfy many: the same patch report supports CIS 7.3, ISO A.8.8, PCI DSS 6.3.3 and CSF PR.PS-02. The MC-03 example: the newest security update dated September 16 was 52 days old on the test date → FAIL; without the filter on security updates, an ordinary update of October 28 (10 days old) would have produced a false PASS.

5.4 Risk analysis fundamentals — impact and likelihood

ISO 31000:2018 defines risk as the effect of uncertainty on objectives; in practice a risk is an event described by its likelihood and its impact (consequence). The event happens when a threat exploits a vulnerability of an asset. A control modifies risk; inherent risk is before controls, residual risk after them; a compensating control reduces the same risk when the expected control is missing (a firewall rule limiting SSH to the administration subnet when the SSH configuration is weak).

Three kinds of analysis: qualitative (ordered levels in words), semi-quantitative (numbers given to the levels and combined, usually multiplied — the course 3×3 matrix), quantitative (money): SLE (single loss expectancy) = asset value × exposure factor, ARO (annualized rate of occurrence) = events per year, ALE (annualized loss expectancy) = SLE × ARO. Example: one day of online sales = $12,000; an outage loses half a day → SLE $6,000; expected once every two years → ARO 0.5 → ALE $3,000 per year; a control costing less than that is economically justified. NIST SP 800-30 Rev. 1 uses five levels.

The course 3×3 matrix: likelihood (1–3) × impact (1–3); score-to-level rule: 1–2 Low, 3–4 Medium, 6 High, 9 Critical.

Level Likelihood — one line Impact — one line
1 Low Needs physical access or several unlikely conditions, or a strong compensating control exists Inconvenience only; no legal duty affected
2 Medium Needs internal network access or a specific condition, or only a partial compensating control A process degraded for more than a day with a workaround, or internal data exposed
3 High Reachable from the Internet or by any employee, already observed or exploited, no compensating control A legal duty breached (PIPEDA, Law 25, PCI DSS) or a High-impact process stopped

The impact scale reuses the Day 4 business-impact criteria, so a finding on a Restricted asset with the PI or CHD flag usually reaches impact 3. Exposure and compensating controls are likelihood factors; legal duties and the asset's business impact are impact factors.

Rating a finding: four elements — condition (what was observed), criterion (the requirement), cause, effect — then five steps: identify the asset and its impact in the inventory → choose the likelihood → choose the impact → compute score and level → write a one-sentence justification quoting the scale. Calibration: several auditors rate the same findings and compare; equal scores → the finding with a legal or contractual deadline comes first. The seven practice findings: F1 SSH root login allowed on the WEB-APP01 stand-in 3×3 = 9 Critical · F2 two accounts inactive > 90 days 2×3 = 6 High · F3 RDP reachable from the office VLAN to the domain controller 2×3 = 6 High · F4 Defender signatures 9 days old 2×2 = 4 Medium · F5 Access Control Policy still a draft 2×2 = 4 Medium · F6 payment provider AOC older than 12 months (PCI DSS 12.8.4) 1×3 = 3 Medium with a legal deadline · F7 BitLocker off on a lab VM without business data 1×1 = 1 Low. Distribution: 1 Critical, 2 High, 3 Medium, 1 Low.

Risk is used before testing (risk-based planning: critical assets first, with more depth and coverage), during and after (every nonconformity rated; the rating orders the findings table and the recommendations). ISO/IEC 27005:2022 names four risk treatment options: modification (add or improve controls), retention (accept with a documented decision), avoidance (stop the activity), sharing (insurance, contract). The auditor rates and reports; the risk owner — usually the asset owner — decides.

Case 1

Ten laptops, one screen-lock requirement

Chapter 1 · Lessons 1.1, 1.3 and 1.4

Scenario

Under the CEO (Chief Executive Officer)'s written mandate, the audit team tests the requirement "laptops lock automatically after 15 minutes of inactivity" (Acceptable Use Policy §4.2; CIS — Center for Internet Security — Windows 11 Benchmark: machine inactivity limit of 900 seconds or fewer). The team reads the registry value InactivityTimeoutSecs on 10 of the 45 laptops and keeps a dated text file per laptop. Results: eight laptops show 900, one shows 1,200 and one shows no value at all. The IT (information technology) administrator explains that the setting is pushed by Intune, but the laptop with no value was re-imaged last month and never re-enrolled.

Questions

  1. Name the five links of the assessment chain in this scenario.
  2. Which type of audit is this, and why?
  3. Is the result a major non-conformity, a minor non-conformity or an observation? Justify.
  4. Give one correction and one corrective action for the laptop with no value.
  5. What must the corrective-action register entry contain?

Most probable answers

  1. Requirement: lock after 15 minutes (policy §4.2, Benchmark ≤ 900 s). Control: the registry value InactivityTimeoutSecs, enforced through Intune. Test: read the value on each sampled laptop and compare it with 900. Evidence: the ten dated text files naming the machine, the value observed and the person who collected it. Finding: eight conform, two non-conform.
  2. A first-party (internal) audit: the organization's own audit team tests the organization itself under the CEO's authorization. The output is an internal report, credible to management but not to outsiders.
  3. A minor non-conformity. The control exists and works on most of the fleet (eight of ten, pushed by Intune); the two failures are isolated lapses in a working control. It would be major only if no timeout policy existed at all in Intune (control absent or broken down). The 1,200-second laptop is a lapse of the same kind; a note that re-imaged laptops bypass enrolment could be added as an observation on the re-imaging procedure.
  4. Correction: re-enroll the re-imaged laptop in Intune (or set the value by hand) so that it locks after 900 seconds today. Corrective action: change the re-imaging procedure so that enrolment in Intune is a mandatory, checked step before a laptop is handed back — this removes the cause and prevents recurrence — then re-test after 30 days.
  5. The non-conformity it addresses, an owner, a due date, the verification method and a re-test date (ISO/IEC 27001:2022 clause 10.2: react, find the cause, act, review effectiveness, document). Tool preferences and prices do not belong in the register.

Scenario

  3. No. It conflicts with privacy by default (Law 25 s. 9.1 — the most protective setting must apply without any action by the person), with the express-consent rule of CASL (a pre-checked box is not an active choice — CRTC bulletin 2012-548) and with meaningful consent under PIPEDA (s. 6.1 and the OPC guidelines, which expect express consent for marketing uses the person would not expect). Case 3

A phished mailbox

Chapter 2 · Lessons 2.2, 2.3 and 2.4; Chapter 3 · Lesson 3.6

Scenario

On Monday, November 16, 2026, at 09:40 Montréal time, the MSP (managed service provider) confirms that an attacker used a phished password to read the mailbox of a store manager for about two days. The mailbox contained e-mail exchanges with 320 customers (names, e-mail addresses, order numbers, two home addresses), including 14 customers who live in France. Questions

  1. Under PIPEDA, which two factors decide whether there is a real risk of significant harm, and what would you conclude here?
  2. Which duties follow under PIPEDA and under Law 25, and towards whom?
  3. Does the GDPR apply to this incident? If yes, what is the deadline and whom do you notify?
  4. How long must the records of this incident be kept, and under which rules?
  5. Which earlier audit item does the MFA exclusion relate to, and what kind of finding is it?

Most probable answers

  1. The sensitivity of the information and the probability of misuse. Sensitivity is low to medium (contact details and order numbers; two home addresses); probability is high (a deliberate attacker read the mailbox for two days). The course rule treats "both factors at least medium" as a RROSH (real risk of significant harm) — the conclusion is yes, mainly because of the deliberate access; phishing of the customers themselves is a plausible misuse.
  2. PIPEDA: report to the OPC as soon as feasible (s. 10.1(1)), notify the affected individuals directly (s. 10.1(3)), notify other organizations that can reduce the harm if useful (s. 10.2), and record the breach whatever the conclusion (s. 10.3). Law 25: take measures to reduce the injury and prevent recurrence, assess the risk of serious injury with three factors (sensitivity, anticipated consequences, likelihood of injurious use — s. 3.7), notify the CAI and the persons concerned promptly if the risk is serious (s. 3.5), and enter the incident in the register of confidentiality incidents (s. 3.8).
  3. Yes, for the 14 customers in France: the web store offers goods to people in the EU (Art. 3(2)(a)), so their data is covered. Article 33: notify the supervisory authority without undue delay and, where feasible, within 72 hours of awareness — Monday 09:40 + 72 h = Thursday, November 19, 09:40 Montréal time (15:40 in Paris). Without an EU establishment there is no lead authority, so the notification goes to the CNIL (the French authority). If the risk to those people is high, they are informed too (Art. 34). Every breach is documented (Art. 33(5)).
  4. The PIPEDA breach record: 24 months after the day the breach was determined to have occurred (Breach of Security Safeguards Regulations, s. 6) → until November 16, 2028. Essay 1 Chapter 1 · Lesson 1.1 Definition and objectives
Using any requirement of the Érable Cycles case file other than the screen lock, name and explain the five links of the assessment chain, and say which link an auditor is never allowed to invent.
Hint. Requirement, control, test, evidence, finding — and remember what an evidence gap means.
Model answer.

Take the requirement "security updates are installed within 30 days" (Patch Management Procedure; PCI DSS 6.3.3; CIS Safeguard 7.3). The requirement is the written expectation with its source and a numeric threshold (30 days). The control is the mechanism meant to satisfy it: Windows Update managed by Intune on the laptops. The test is the planned procedure: run Get-HotFix on a sample of laptops, keep only security updates, compute the age of the newest one. The evidence is the verifiable record the test produces: the dated CSV (comma-separated values) export per machine with its SHA-256 fingerprint and the hostname. The finding is the evaluation of that evidence against the threshold: PASS, or a non-conformity if the newest security update is older than 30 days — minor if one laptop lags, major if no patching process exists. The link an auditor never invents is the evidence: "Not tested — reason: …" is an acceptable record; a plausible number written in place of a missing result is academic misconduct and breaks the audit trail.

Essay 2 Chapter 1 · Lesson 1.3 Types of audits and assessments
Compare first-party, second-party, third-party and regulatory audits in terms of who performs them, their independence, their typical output and the consequences they can carry. Give one Érable Cycles example for each.
Hint. Independence rises from left to right, and so does the weight of the result.
Model answer.

A first-party (internal) audit is performed by the organization on itself; independence is lowest, the output is an internal report credible to management, and the consequence is a corrective-action plan — the audit team checking the company against CIS Controls IG1 under the CEO's mandate. A second-party audit is performed by an interested party, usually a customer; independence is medium, the output is a questionnaire result or a site-visit report, and the consequence is winning or losing a contract — the corporate client's 40-question questionnaire before a fleet purchase. A third-party audit is performed by an independent body with no stake in the result (a certification body or a PCI QSA); independence is high, the output is a certificate or an attestation, and the consequence is granting, refusing or withdrawing that certificate — an accredited body auditing the ISMS for an ISO/IEC 27001 certificate. A regulatory audit is performed by a public authority (OPC, CAI, CRTC); independence is high and, unlike a certification body, the regulator has legal powers: binding orders, administrative monetary penalties, prosecution — the CAI investigating how newsletter consents were collected after a complaint. The PCI DSS SAQ sits between the first two: a self-declaration, first party in form, contractual in origin, validated by the acquirer.

Essay 3 Chapter 1 · Lesson 1.4 The role of conformity assessment in continuous improvement
Explain what happens after a non-conformity is found, using PDCA and ISO/IEC 27001 clause 10.2. Distinguish correction, corrective action and verification of effectiveness, and illustrate with historical item H5 (backup restore test).
Hint. The assessment is Check; everything after it is Act.
Model answer.

The conformity assessment is the Check step of PDCA; the non-conformity opens the Act step, which clause 10.2 structures: react and correct, evaluate the need to eliminate the cause, implement the action, review its effectiveness, update risks and the ISMS, and keep documented information. For H5, the correction is to run a restore test now and record it. The root cause is that nobody owns the quarterly test and no schedule exists, which is why a control that worked last year stopped — a regression. The corrective action removes that cause: assign the IT administrator as owner, put the quarterly test in the calendar with the MSP, define the record to keep (restored files, date, result). The verification of effectiveness is the re-test, 30 days after the due date: the auditor checks that a dated restore-test record exists and that the test succeeded. Only then is the register entry closed. Management reviews the status in the clause 9.3 management review, and next year's historical comparison shows whether H5 moved from "regressed" back to "conform".

Essay 4 Chapter 1 · Lessons 1.5 and 1.6; Chapter 5 · Lesson 5.5
Why does the course repeat "no mandate, no test"? Give the legal reason, the professional reason and what the mandate must contain.
Hint. Criminal Code, ISO 19011 principles, Annex D of the Legal Notice.
Model answer.

The legal reason: accessing or using a computer system without authorization is a criminal offence in Canada (Criminal Code s. 342.1; similar laws exist in the United States and the EU), and the intention to learn is not a defence; host discovery or configuration tests on systems you do not own are reconnaissance of somebody else's systems. The written mandate is what makes the tests lawful: it comes from the person with authority over the systems (the CEO or the asset owner, not the IT administrator alone), and it states the systems, the allowed activities, the exclusions, the period, a contact, a signature and a date (Annex D of the Legal Notice; section 6.1). The professional reason: the ISO 19011 principles — integrity, due professional care, independence, confidentiality — require the auditor to act within an agreed scope, to protect the systems under test (ISO/IEC 27002 control 8.34: read-only access where possible, availability-affecting tests outside business hours, logged access) and to keep results confidential. In the course, Labs 0 to 2 run under the instructor's course mandate and Lab 3 under the CEO authorization letter you adapt and sign in Lab 2 Part B; the only real assets you ever touch are your two VMs on your own lab subnet.

Essay 5 Chapter 2 · Lessons 2.1 and 2.5
PCI DSS is not a law. Explain why it is nevertheless mandatory for Érable Cycles, where it sits in the hierarchy of obligations, who enforces it and what the consequences of non-compliance are. Is there a way for the company to make PCI DSS not apply?
Hint. Follow the chain card brands → acquirer → merchant agreement.
Model answer.

PCI DSS is published by the PCI Security Standards Council, founded by the card brands. The brands require it from the acquirers (the banks that process card payments), and each acquirer writes compliance into the merchant agreement it signs with a merchant. That signature is what binds Érable Cycles: the standard becomes a level-2 contractual obligation, below laws and regulations but above the voluntary frameworks and the internal policies. It is enforced by the acquirer and the card brands, not by a regulator: fees and fines passed on by the acquirer, higher transaction costs and, in the end, the loss of the right to accept cards. The only way to make it not apply is to stop accepting payment cards altogether, which no retailer wants. What the company can do is reduce the scope: with a hosted payment page online and P2PE terminals in the stores, no card number reaches its systems, most systems are out of the cardholder data environment, and validation shrinks to SAQ A and SAQ P2PE with an Attestation of Compliance, instead of the full SAQ D.

Essay 6 Chapter 2 · Lesson 2.2 PIPEDA · §2.5 Breaches of security safeguards
Describe the real-risk-of-significant-harm test and the duties that follow a breach of security safeguards under PIPEDA. What is recorded, by whom, and for how long? What is the penalty for ignoring these duties?
Hint. Two factors, four duties, 24 months, $100,000.
Model answer.

A breach of security safeguards is the loss of, or unauthorized access to or disclosure of, personal information because safeguards failed or were never established. The organization first contains the breach, then assesses the RROSH with two factors fixed by the law: the sensitivity of the information involved and the probability that it has been, is being or will be misused; significant harm includes humiliation, damage to reputation or relationships, financial loss, identity theft, loss of employment or business opportunities and damage to property. If a real risk exists, four duties follow: report to the OPC as soon as feasible (s. 10.1(1), using the OPC form), notify the affected individuals as soon as feasible, directly in most cases (s. 10.1(3)), notify other organizations or government institutions that can reduce the harm, such as the police (s. 10.2), and — for every breach, whatever the conclusion — record it (s. 10.3). The record is kept by the organization for 24 months after the day it determined the breach occurred (SOR/2018-64, s. 6) and given to the OPC on request. Knowingly failing to report, notify or keep records is an offence: up to $10,000 on summary conviction or $100,000 on indictment (s. 28). In the stolen-laptop example the assessment concluded RROSH = yes mainly because the laptop was not encrypted — a principle 4.7 safeguard failure — so all four duties applied.

Essay 8 Chapter 2 · Lesson 2.4 The GDPR · §4.1 and §4.3
Explain why the GDPR applies to Érable Cycles, what "Partly" means in the Lab 1 register, and which authority must be notified after a personal-data breach. The company becomes aware of a breach on Friday, November 20, 2026 at 17:30 Montréal time: when does the 72-hour period end, and what happens if the notification is late?
Hint. Article 3(2)(a), Recital 23, no one-stop shop, calendar hours.
Model answer.

Érable Cycles has no establishment in the EU, so Art. 3(1) does not apply; Art. 3(2)(a) does, because the company offers goods to people in the EU: it delivers to France and Belgium, displays prices in euros and offers a French checkout — three of the Recital 23 signs that distinguish an offer from a website that merely happens to be reachable from Europe. The regulation therefore covers only the personal data of those customers, not the data of Canadian customers or employees — that is what "Partly" means in the applicability register, with that sentence as the reason. Because there is no EU establishment, the one-stop shop does not apply: a breach is notified to the supervisory authority of each member state where affected customers live — the CNIL for France, the APD-GBA for Belgium. Article 33 requires notification without undue delay and, where feasible, within 72 calendar hours of awareness, weekends included: Friday 17:30 + 72 h = Monday, November 23, 2026 at 17:30 Montréal time (23:30 in Paris). A late notification must explain the reasons for the delay; every breach, notified or not, is documented (Art. 33(5)); if the risk to the people is high, they are informed too (Art. 34). Failing these duties falls in the lower fine tier (€10 million or 2 %), but a breach that also reveals a violation of the principles or the lawful bases can reach the upper tier (€20 million or 4 %).

Essay 9 Chapter 2 · Lesson 2.5 PCI DSS v4.0.1 · §5.3 and §5.4; Chapter 4 · Lesson 4.5
Define the cardholder data environment and the PCI DSS scope. Explain how a hosted payment page and P2PE terminals reduce the scope of Érable Cycles, which SAQs result, and how this appears in the asset inventory.
Hint. Scope = CDE + connected systems; follow the path of the card data; CHD flag.
Model answer.

The CDE is made of the people, processes and technologies that store, process or transmit cardholder data or sensitive authentication data. The PCI DSS scope is the CDE plus every system connected to it or able to affect its security (for example a domain controller that manages the accounts of CDE systems); segmentation — firewall rules between VLANs — keeps other systems out of scope. Online, the hosted payment page means the customer types the card number on a page served by the payment provider; the web server never receives it. In the stores, P2PE terminals encrypt the card data inside a validated terminal and only the solution provider can decrypt it, so the store network carries only encrypted data. Because no card number is stored anywhere, the database and the backups also leave the CDE. The result: validation with SAQ A for the web store (card-not-present, all account-data functions outsourced; since January 2025 with the confirmation about script attacks) and SAQ P2PE for the stores, each with an Attestation of Compliance — instead of SAQ D, which applied two years ago when the web store had its own card form. Essay 10 Chapter 2 · Lesson 2.6 CASL · §6.2 and §6.3
A customer bought an e-bike on October 5, 2024 and never ticked the newsletter box; another customer gave express consent in 2021. Explain the three requirements of every commercial electronic message, the two kinds of consent with their time limits, the proof-of-consent rule, and whether each customer may receive the newsletter of December 1, 2026.
Hint. Consent, identification, unsubscribe; 2 years / 6 months; no expiry for express consent; s. 13.
Model answer.

Every CEM must satisfy three requirements (s. 6): consent, express or implied; identification of the sender and of anyone on whose behalf the message is sent, with contact information — a mailing address and a phone number, e-mail address or web address — valid at least 60 days; and an unsubscribe mechanism, free and easy, valid at least 60 days, given effect within 10 business days (s. 11). Express consent is an active, informed agreement (an empty box ticked, a signed form, a recorded oral yes — never a pre-checked box) and has no expiry until the person withdraws it. Implied consent exists only in situations listed by the law, mainly the existing business relationship: a purchase within the past two years or an inquiry within the past six months. The sender bears the burden of proof (s. 13) and keeps the date, the method, the wording and the source of every consent. The first customer's implied consent ran from October 5, 2024 to October 5, 2026; on December 1, 2026 it has expired, so the newsletter must not be sent unless express consent was obtained in the meantime — the lesson of the example is to ask for express consent before the two years run out. The second customer's express consent of 2021 is still valid in December 2026 as long as no unsubscribe request was received, so the newsletter may be sent, provided the identification and unsubscribe requirements are met. The maximum administrative penalty per violation is $1 million for an individual and $10 million for a business (CRTC).

Essay 11 Chapter 3 · Lesson 3.1 NIST CSF 2.0 · §1.4 and §1.5
Explain what a Current Profile, a Target Profile and a gap are, why the course calls an Organizational Profile "a small conformity assessment", and what the CSF does not give Érable Cycles.
Hint. Requirement, actual situation, comparison, action — and the three things the CSF is not.
Model answer.

An Organizational Profile is the CSF Core applied to one organization. The Current Profile records which outcomes (Subcategories such as GV.PO-01 or PR.AA-05) are achieved today and to what degree, based on evidence; the Target Profile records the outcomes the organization wants to achieve, chosen from its legal obligations (Lab 1), its risks and its business goals; the gap is target minus current for each outcome, and the gaps ranked by size become the action plan — the example scored seven Subcategories on a 0–4 scale the team defined itself (the CSF imposes none). A Profile is a small conformity assessment because it contains the same four elements as an audit finding: a requirement (the Subcategory), a statement of the actual situation (evidence), a comparison (the gap) and an action. The CSF gives a common vocabulary with the CEO and the MSP, a structure for reporting, free Informative References to detailed sources (SP 800-53, CIS Controls, ISO/IEC 27001 through OLIR and the CPRT) and a way to show progress year after year. It does not give configuration settings (no password length, no firewall rule — that is the role of CIS Benchmarks and Microsoft baselines), it does not give a certification (nobody is "CSF-certified") and it is not a legal obligation (the laws are PIPEDA, Law 25, the GDPR and CASL; the contract is PCI DSS). That is why Lab 1 pairs a CSF Profile with CIS Controls IG1.

Essay 13 Chapter 3 · Lesson 3.4 Data retention policies
Explain the difference between a legal minimum and a legal maximum retention period, give two examples of each that apply to Érable Cycles, say which one prevails when they conflict, and describe the two retention non-conformities an auditor looks for.
Hint. Keep at least / keep no longer than; the minimum wins while it lasts.
Model answer.

A legal minimum says "keep at least this long": the record must exist for later verification. Examples: tax and accounting records six years from the end of the last tax year (CRA and Revenu Québec); audit logs at least 12 months with the last three months immediately available (PCI DSS 10.5.1); PIPEDA breach records 24 months; the Law 25 incident register five years. A legal maximum says "keep no longer than necessary for the purpose": the privacy laws apply it to every record that contains personal information — PIPEDA Principle 4.5, Law 25 section 23 (destroy or anonymize once the purposes are achieved), GDPR Article 5(1)(e) storage limitation; PCI DSS 3.2.1 adds a maximum for account data with a check every three months. One record can be under both: an invoice is a tax record and holds the customer's name and address. When they conflict, the minimum wins for as long as it lasts, because the privacy laws themselves except retention periods fixed by another law; when it ends, the maximum applies and the record must be destroyed or anonymized. The auditor looks for two non-conformities: destroyed too early — below a minimum, such as the pfSense firewall overwriting its logs after 30 days or invoices shredded after three years — and kept too long — past the purpose without a legal basis, such as customer accounts never deleted or a newsletter list with addresses collected ten years ago. The first kind is found in policies and configurations (logrotate, event-log sizes); the second in the practices described during the document review.

Essay 14 Chapter 3 · Lessons 3.5 and 3.6
Describe one line of the traceability matrix, explain the three gap types with an Érable Cycles example each, and explain the sentence "if it isn't documented, it didn't happen" using the ISO 19011 qualities of evidence.
Hint. Requirement → policy clause → control → evidence → test; sufficient, reliable, relevant.
Model answer.

One matrix line keeps five elements together for one requirement: the requirement with its source (PIPEDA 4.5 / Law 25 s. 23 — personal information kept only as long as necessary), the policy clause that states it (Privacy Policy §6: accounts inactive for 24 months are deleted), the control that enforces it (a monthly deletion job in the web store), the evidence that proves it (the job's log, a count of deleted accounts) and the test the auditor applies (re-performance of the query, observation of the job), plus the crosswalk references (CIS 3.4, CSF GV.PO-01 / PR.DS, ISO A.5.33 and A.8.10). Reading the line exposes three gaps. A policy gap: a requirement with no approved clause — the 12-character password rule rests only on the draft Access Control Policy, so it is unsupported even though Active Directory enforces it. An implementation gap: a clause with no control — the Privacy Policy promises deletion after 24 months but no job exists. An evidence gap: a control with no proof — backups run every night but no restore test was ever recorded, so the auditor treats the control as not operating. The sentence "if it isn't documented, it didn't happen" is the definition of audit evidence, not cynicism: ISO 19011 requires evidence to be sufficient (enough of it), reliable (from a trustworthy source — a system output or a signed record ranks above a verbal statement) and relevant (about the requirement tested). An activity that left no record cannot meet any of the three, so the auditor cannot credit it; that is also why every evidence file in the course has a unique name in an evidence index and why "Not tested — reason" is written rather than a guess.

Essay 15 Chapter 4 · Lessons 4.1 and 4.2
Explain the three problems an asset inventory solves in an audit, then describe the twelve columns of the course template in their three groups and say which requirement each group satisfies.
Hint. Scope errors, unowned assets, wrong protection levels; identification, classification, maintenance.
Model answer.

Without an inventory, (1) scope errors happen — a system nobody listed is never tested, like the backup NAS that was missing from last year's scope so its restore test was never checked; (2) assets are unowned — when a finding is raised nobody is accountable for the corrective action; (3) protection levels are wrong — a laptop holding customer records is protected like a kiosk because nobody recorded that it holds personal information. Essay 16 Chapter 4 · Lessons 4.3 and 4.5
Explain how the course finds the critical assets (business impact analysis, single points of failure) and how the decision table classifies an asset. Apply both to DB-RDS01 and to NAS-BKP01.

Essay 17 Chapter 4 · Lessons 4.4 and 4.6
State the discovery rule of the course and explain the three tests an auditor applies to an inventory. Define drift and say why a retired asset is never deleted from the inventory.
Hint. Own subnet, own VMs, nothing else; document test, two reconciliations; change ticket; ISO 5.11 and 7.14.
Model answer.

The discovery rule: nmap -sn (host discovery, no port scan) is run only against the subnet of your two lab VMs — for example 192.168.56.0/24 on a host-only network — from inside one of the VMs and under the written mandate; the expected result is three or four hosts (the host adapter, the hypervisor's DHCP server, your two VMs), and any other result means you are on the wrong network and must stop. Sent to a network you do not control, discovery is reconnaissance of somebody else's systems (Criminal Code s. 342.1). The auditor tests the inventory three ways. The document test: version number, review date, reviewer and approval are present on the first page and the review dates respect the cadence (CIS 1.1 twice a year, PCI DSS 12.5.2 yearly, the course quarterly). Reconciliation from the inventory to reality: a sample of records (three of twelve) is checked against the assets — LT-017 exists, sits where the record says, has the recorded owner. Reconciliation from reality to the inventory: every host seen by nmap -sn or by Intune is found in the records; a host with no record is an unauthorized asset (CIS Safeguard 1.2) to identify and handle. Drift is a change in an asset between two reviews — new software, a new address — with no change ticket: either an undocumented change or an unauthorized installation (CIS 2.3), a finding in both cases. A decommissioned asset is marked retired with the date and the disposal evidence rather than deleted, because the auditor asks what happened to the data it held (ISO controls 5.11 return of assets and 7.14 secure disposal) and the version history must remain reconstructable.

Essay 18 Chapter 5 · Lessons 5.1 and 5.2
List the five parts of a test, explain what makes a pass rule measurable and the difference between a test of design and a test of operating effectiveness, then write a complete test definition for MC-03 (operating-system patch level) on LAB-WIN11.
Hint. Requirement, method, sample, expected result, evidence; a number, a period, a file name.
Model answer.

Before execution a test has five parts: the requirement (criterion), the method, the sample, the expected result (pass rule) and the evidence to keep; after execution the auditor adds the actual result, the verdict (PASS, FAIL, N/A with a reason, Not tested with a reason) and the evidence reference. A pass rule is measurable when it can be judged true or false without opinion — "accounts lock after at most 10 failed attempts for at least 30 minutes" is measurable, "passwords are strong" is not. A test of design checks that the control as described would meet the requirement (the Patch Management Procedure requires security updates within 30 days); a test of operating effectiveness checks that it actually operated over a period (the update history shows one security update each month, each installed within 30 days); one screenshot proves existence at one moment, not operation over time. Test definition for MC-03: Requirement — security updates installed within 30 days of release (Patch Management Procedure; PCI DSS 6.3.3; CIS Safeguard 7.3; ISO A.8.8; CSF PR.PS-02). Method — re-performance with Get-HotFix by the auditor, plus document review of the procedure. Sample — LAB-WIN11 as the stand-in for the Windows fleet, all installed updates; in the full audit, 10 of 45 laptops (5 judgement + 5 random, seed 20261107). Expected result — the newest security update is at most 30 days old on the test date, and at least one security update per month appears in the last 90 days. Evidence — L3-E07_MC-03_hotfix_LAB-WIN11.csv with its SHA-256 fingerprint and the completed test record. In the example the newest security update was 52 days old → FAIL; without the filter on security updates an ordinary update 10 days old would have produced a false PASS.

Essay 19 Chapter 5 · Lesson 5.4 Risk analysis fundamentals
Describe the course 3×3 risk matrix (scales, score-to-level rule), the four elements used to write a finding and the four risk treatment options. Rate the finding "RDP (Remote Desktop Protocol) port 3389 is reachable from the office VLAN to the domain controller" and say who decides how to treat it.
Hint. 1–2 Low, 3–4 Medium, 6 High, 9 Critical; condition, criterion, cause, effect; modification, retention, avoidance, sharing.
Model answer.

The matrix multiplies a likelihood level (1 needs physical access or several unlikely conditions or a strong compensating control; 2 needs internal network access or a specific condition or only a partial compensating control; 3 reachable from the Internet or by any employee, already observed, no compensating control) by an impact level (1 inconvenience, no legal duty; 2 a process degraded for more than a day with a workaround or internal data exposed; 3 a legal duty breached or a High-impact process stopped). The score-to-level rule: 1–2 Low, 3–4 Medium, 6 High, 9 Critical; the impact scale reuses the Day 4 business-impact criteria so that inventory and risk stay consistent. A finding is written with four elements before it is rated: the condition (what was observed), the criterion (the requirement), the cause (why the gap exists) and the effect (what can happen); then five steps: find the asset and its impact in the inventory, choose the likelihood, choose the impact, compute the score, write a one-sentence justification quoting the scales. For the RDP finding (practice finding F3): condition — port 3389 open from the office VLAN to SRV-DC01; criterion — MC-19 segmentation and firewall rules, PCI DSS 1.x, CIS 12.x, ISO A.8.20–A.8.22; cause — no firewall rule restricting administration to a management subnet; effect — any compromised office laptop can attack the only domain controller. Likelihood 2 (needs internal network access), impact 3 (a Restricted single point of failure that supports in-store sales and holds credentials): 2 × 3 = 6, High. ISO/IEC 27005:2022 gives four treatment options: modification (add or improve controls — restrict RDP to the administration subnet, require MFA), retention (accept with a documented decision), avoidance (stop the activity) and sharing (insurance, a contract). The auditor rates and reports; the risk owner — the asset owner identified on Day 4, here the IT administrator as infrastructure owner, with the CEO for residual risk — decides.
